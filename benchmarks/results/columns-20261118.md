# Columnar scene and overview against JSON (ADR 0019, step 4, 2026-11-18)

Raw data: [`columns-20261118T134607Z.jsonl`](columns-20261118T134607Z.jsonl), written by
`MAXLOAD=4 WAIT=900 sh benchmarks/protocol/measure-columns.sh 5` (`benchmarks/protocol/measure-columns.mjs`).

Apple M3 (8 cores), Darwin, rustc 1.99.0, Node 24.21.0, revision 01185e1 with uncommitted changes (the columnar overview
and this script; the built WASM contained them). **The machine was not quiet: load average 17 at the start and 17 at the
end** (other jobs; it never fell below 4 within the 15 minutes the script waits). Medians of 5 runs after one warm-up
(3 for the overview), single run per size, so differences of a few tens of percent are not established; factors of 5 or
more are. Graphs: `benchmarks/overview/generate.py --seed 1` at 10 000 and 100 000 nodes, import limit 10 000.

Path measured: the engine call in WASM (Node), then the answer to the page through a worker (Node `worker_threads`).
JSON: parsed, then structured-cloned. Columns: copied once out of the module's memory, transferred. "Views" is a host that
calls the engine directly and reads typed-array views (nothing copied, nothing moved).

## Scene of a resident session

| nodes | view | boxes / routes | JSON scene (ms) | columns, transferred (ms) | columns, views (ms) | JSON bytes | column bytes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10 000 | depth 1, one container opened | 107 / 781 | 49.9 | 5.0 | 1.9 | 571 030 | 67 853 |
| 100 000 | depth 1, one container opened | 521 / 8 570 | 519.2 | 42.1 | 33.1 | 7 029 003 | 833 292 |
| 10 000 | full view, no abstraction | 10 000 / 15 111 | 2 079.4 | 941.3 | 949.3 | 12 672 694 | 1 443 480 |
| 100 000 | full view, no abstraction | 12 714 / 11 831 | 1 032.0 | 538.3 | 571.6 | 12 967 102 | 1 408 696 |

- **Size:** columns are 8.4 to 9.2 times smaller than the JSON scene in every view (ids once in a table, numbers as `f32`).
- **Time:** where the view is small the transfer dominated and columns are 10 to 12 times faster (49.9 → 5.0 ms,
  519 → 42 ms). In the full views the time is mostly the engine building the scene (`build_scene`, which both paths do),
  and columns are 1.9 to 2.2 times faster. Without the worker (views) the numbers are the same, so the gain is the missing
  serialization and parsing, not the transfer.
- What is not measured: a renderer reading the columns, ports, labels and rows (not columns yet), Chrome.

## Overview (a session with a resident graph)

| nodes | request | JSON arrays (ms) | typed arrays, transferred (ms) | JSON bytes | typed bytes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10 000 | whole answer for a new graph | 924.1 | 765.7 | 692 849 | 865 963 |
| 10 000 | continuation (`positionsOnly`) | 9.9 | 5.5 | 183 066 | 245 702 |
| 100 000 | whole answer for a new graph | 8 500.9 | 10 386.2 | 7 749 501 | 8 789 377 |
| 100 000 | continuation (`positionsOnly`) | 232.4 | 63.8 | 1 917 068 | 2 454 307 |

- **The whole answer is dominated by the layout** (seconds at 100 000 nodes), identical in both modes; the differences
  in both directions (924 vs 766 ms, 8 501 vs 10 386 ms) are load noise, not an effect. Nothing here supports a claim
  for the whole answer.
- **A continuation** is 1.8 times (10 000 nodes) and 3.6 times (100 000 nodes) faster with typed arrays, which is the
  case that matters for a live view (every settle step after a drop). Its layout work is the same in both modes, so the
  difference is the missing serialization and parsing of three arrays; with this load it is one measurement per size.
- **The typed answer is larger, not smaller** (+25 % whole, +34 % continuation): the JSON positions are rounded to
  hundredths and short, small integers are as short in JSON as in binary, while the columns hold `f64` (positions) and
  4-byte integers. Size is therefore no argument for columns of the overview; the gain is time. `f64` is kept so that a
  viewer finds the same chains in both (a test compares models and vertex buffers); `f32` would halve the positions but
  can merge discs 0.01 apart beyond about 1.3 · 10^5. Positions as `i32` hundredths would be exact and about a third
  smaller than the JSON; that is a candidate for the binary frames of step 5, where bytes matter (over a network), not
  here, where the buffers are transferred without a copy.
